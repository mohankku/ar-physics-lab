Measure g from a real drop

  1. Set up. Stand the camera on a shelf or table, about 2 m from a plain wall, so the whole drop (top of the frame to the floor) is in view.
  2. Pick your ball. A brightly colored ball works best (red against a white wall is ideal). Choose its color and its type below; its known size sets the scale.
  3. Open camera, then press Arm.
  4. Hold the ball still near the top of the frame. The ring on it turns from orange to green when it's ready.
  5. Let go. Don't throw it, just open your fingers. Recording starts and stops by itself.
  6. Read g below. The fall replays in slow motion beside a ghost ball falling at 9.81 m/s²; repeat a few times and compare.

Catch & drop a virtual ball

  1. Open camera and step back so your hand and the floor-to-ceiling space are in view. The first time, the hand tracker downloads (about 18 MB). No camera? Press Use mouse / touch: press and hold is a fist.
  2. Show an open hand ✋. A green outline means the camera sees it.
  3. Make a fist ✊ on the ball to catch it. It moves with your hand.
  4. Raise it, then open your hand. The ball falls from that height and the fall is timed.
  5. Catch it again with a fist where it's falling (slow motion helps!), or pick it up from the floor. Drop from different heights and watch the table work out g.
How it works

A hand-tracking model finds 21 points on your hand in each camera frame, on this device. A hand counts as closed when the fingertips come back close to the knuckles. The ball falls from rest with h = ½·g·t², so t² = (2/g)·h: on the graph, drops from different heights line up on a straight line whose slope gives g. Set View height to how many meters the camera sees from the bottom of the picture to the top, so heights are right.

Theory. From rest, free fall covers y = ½·g·t², so a drop of height h takes t = √(2h/g) and gravity follows as g = 2h/t². Near Earth's surface g ≈ 9.81 m/s². Galileo's insight: without air resistance, the mass of the ball doesn't matter.

Exp 02 — Projectile Motion: one throw, two motions

Auto-track a thrown ball

Stand the camera to one side so it looks straight across the path of the throw, with a plain wall behind. Press Arm, hold the ball still in view, then toss it gently across the frame in an arc. Recording starts when the ball moves and stops when it leaves the frame, bounces or is caught. The page separates the two motions: sideways, x = x0 + vx·t (no force, so constant speed); vertically, y = y0 + vy·t − ½·g·t². The frames where your hand is still pushing are dropped automatically. After the fit, the throw replays over the video with its velocity arrows (watch vx stay the same while vy shrinks, flips and grows) next to a ghost thrown the same way at 9.81 m/s². Scale works as in Exp 01: the ball's known size is easiest; stand so the throw happens at the same distance from the camera as the ball you measure.

Theory. Once the ball leaves your hand, the only force on it is gravity, and gravity pulls straight down. So the horizontal velocity vx never changes, while the vertical velocity falls by g every second: vy(t) = vy0 − g·t. The path is a parabola. A ball launched at speed v and angle θ rises v²sin²θ / 2g and lands back at launch height v²sin2θ / g away, which is farthest at 45°. The "sideways accel." readout tests the first claim directly: it should be zero within its uncertainty (air drag makes it slightly negative for fast throws).

Exp 03 — Pendulum Period: measure g with a clock

Time a swinging pendulum

Hang a brightly colored ball from a string, tied to something that won't move. Measure the string from the pivot to the top of the ball; its radius is added for you. Point the camera at the swing from the front, pull the ball a hand's width aside (under about 20°) and let go. Then press Start (or Space). Each pass through the middle is timed, and recording stops by itself after the number of swings you choose. The period T comes from a straight-line fit of those pass times, and g = 4π²L/T², corrected for the swing's size. Because it relies only on the string length and the camera's clock, this is usually the most accurate way to measure g in the lab. Compare it with your drop and throw.

Theory. For small swings, a pendulum of length L (pivot to the ball's center) has period T = 2π√(L/g). The period doesn't depend on the ball's mass, and barely depends on how far it swings, so g = 4π²L/T². Bigger swings are a little slower: at 20° the period is 0.8% longer, which the fit corrects for exactly. Air drag makes the swing die away. The Q readout counts how many swings it takes to lose most of its energy (about Q/π of them for the amplitude to fall to 37%). Timing ten swings, not one, is the classic trick. The fit uses every pass through the middle, so the timing error shrinks the longer it swings.
